02 How the course works

A carefully selected list of TED talks and games forms the "content" of this course. We do not expect students to learn or reproduce this content; rather, the TED talks and games are meant to spark inquiry and discussion. All sprint days (except project days) look like this:

01

TED Talk / Game (45 - 90 mins)

Students watch the TED talk, or participate in the game. They can take notes if they wish to, or in the case of the talk, watch it more than once. Supplementary material might be provided for students to explore before the next activity.

↓
02

Question Generation (45 mins)

Students work individually at first, and then in small groups, to come up with their own questions to ask, in response to the TED talk or game.

↓
03

Question Curation by Instructors (30 mins - break for students)

Instructors curate the questions submitted by students, and create a list of 5-6 shared questions for the cohort.

↓
04

Self-Directed Research (45 - 90 mins)

Students research the curated list of 5-6 questions, in order to come up with their answers, and find supporting evidence. Given the open-ended nature of questions students are encouraged to ask, it is very unlikely that a group of students will approach their answer in the same way.

↓
05

Discussion in Small Groups (45 mins)

Students meet in the Upper Deck (an outdoor seating area) and discuss each question within their small groups. Instructors circulate and observe, joining groups from time to time in order to provide support or pose follow-up questions.

↓
06

Full Class Discussion (45 mins)

An instructor-led conversation with the full class in order to surface different perspectives, elevate interesting conversations from the small-group discussions, reflect on participation and discussion quality and introduce guiding frameworks for the next day.

↓
07

Written Reflection (30 mins)

Each student engages in open-ended reflection about the day, writing down anything that is on top of their minds. Usually, students tend to reflect on their evolving understanding of the content itself, or what happened in their group discussions.

↓

04 Course outcomes

These course outcome statements represent what students should be able to do through each course activity.

01

Question Generation

  • ask questions that are articulate, open-ended, generative and have metacognitive depth
02

Self-Directed Research

  • choose a position, in response to a question
  • gather evidence to support a position
  • judge the quality of different kinds of evidence
03

Small-Group Discussion

  • observe shared norms of discussion
  • explain and reason about a chosen position
  • support a position with evidence
  • judge the quality of an argument
  • engage respectfully across different perspectives
04

Written Reflection

  • reflect on learning, course content, questions generated and discussions

05 Frameworks

Question Generation frameworks help students ideate more broadly when writing questions. Discussion frameworks shape the norms and quality of the small-group discussions. Both are introduced incrementally over the course.

Question Generation frameworks

P.S. Developed primarily for TED talks. Would they also work for games? Open

5W1H

Question starters — What, Who, When, Where, How — to help with blank-canvas syndrome. Can you write questions that begin with each of these starters?

"Connection" words

Words or phrases that connect the TED talk or game to current events or systems in the world, to spark connections.

Assumptions and Consequences

What assumptions does the speaker make? What are the consequences if the speaker is right — what happens in the real world, and who or what does it influence?

So What?

Assume the speaker is right about everything. So what — why should you or I care? If it doesn't affect either of us, who would care? Keep asking "so what" until the question feels interesting or important.

Perspective Taking

Take the perspective of a friend or family member, of people in different professions (a doctor, a migrant labourer, a lawyer, a business owner), or of people in different places or communities. What would they say about this talk?

Questioning Circles

Three domains questions can draw from:
You (personal experience), the Talk (what the speaker says), and the World (the real world, or another talk).
Combine at least two — e.g. You + Talk: "If you were in the speaker's position, would you have reached the same conclusion?" All three can also be combined at once.

06 Activity guides

How each activity runs

This section will house detailed guides that instructors can use to run every activity in the course. Still a work-in-progress. In Progress

Question Generation

Maps to the Question Generation activity · 45 minutes · follows every TED talk and game.

Students work individually and then in groups to generate open-ended questions that will lead to meaningful, interesting discussion. The talks and games are selected to spark inquiry; hence student-generated questions need not focus solely on the topic at hand — allow students to go off on tangents if they wish to!

Prep — before the session

  • Decide the framework of the day and have 1–2 example questions ready that are not drawn from the talk's topic.
  • Confirm the Google Form for question submission is created and shared on LearnX.
  • Check that students are seated in their groups, and that each group has a device-free space to write.
  • Have the talk or game ready to reference, and be prepared to remind students to put devices away.

Individual Question Generation

20 min
  1. Ask students to put away all devices, including phones. Questions should be authentic and channel students' own curiosity.
  2. Students may start writing in their notebooks whenever ready. Remind them to write legibly — peers will read their questions.
  3. Some will start immediately; others may be blank. This is normal — let them sit with the discomfort for at least 5 minutes.
  4. When most of the class has written what first occurs to them, introduce the framework of the day (below), in under 5 minutes.
  5. Describe the framework briefly, with 1–2 examples not drawn from the talk's topic. Allow ~10 more minutes of individual writing.

Silent Reading in Groups

5 min
  1. Students pass notebooks around and silently read peers' questions. They may vote on favourites or make small phrasing edits.
  2. Maintain silence so students focus on reading.
  3. Ends when every student has read every other student's questions in their group.

Group Discussion

20 min
  1. Once silent reading is mostly done, groups may discuss.
  2. Goal: identify the 4 most interesting questions in the group. They may rephrase, aggregate similar questions, or write new ones that emerge.
  3. Remind students not to start answering questions or defending positions. Redirect as often as needed — the task is selecting 4 questions.

Question Submission

5 min
  1. Ensure the Google Form is already shared on LearnX.
  2. One student per group uses a device to submit a single form with all 4 selected questions.

07 Question quality

Question Quality Rubric

Used to gauge the quality of student-submitted questions and select the best for discussion — not for grades. Questions are coded separately on four criteria, each at three levels. Interpret the student's intended meaning as charitably as possible; except for Articulation, score the underlying question rather than its wording; evaluate each criterion independently. The four criteria are the four adjectives in the Question Generation activity statement.

Articulation

The clarity with which the intended question is communicated — whether it can be understood without external clarification. Not grammar, style, or length.

L1 LowCore intent is ambiguous or incomplete; clarification necessary before meaningful discussion.
L2 MedMeaning can be inferred, but unclear references or specialised wording leave multiple plausible interpretations.
L3 HighMeaning is immediately clear and can be understood without clarification.

Openness

The degree to which a question invites multiple defensible interpretations or perspectives — moving from "closed textbook" toward "open intellectual."

L1 LowPrimarily seeks a known fact, verification, or bounded choice.
L2 MedAnswers exist in bodies of research in certain domains; only a few well-reasoned positions are possible.
L3 HighExplores permanently open-ended and contested questions; multiple well-reasoned positions remain possible even after substantial discussion.

Generativity

The extent to which answering the question naturally gives rise to further meaningful inquiry.

L1 LowLargely terminal; once answered, little natural direction for further discussion.
L2 MedNaturally invites one or two follow-up questions within the same line of inquiry.
L3 HighOpens multiple avenues of inquiry; answering it generates several deeper questions or directions.

Metacognitive Depth

The degree to which the question prompts reflection on one's own thinking, assumptions, reasoning, or biases.

L1 LowFocuses entirely on external facts, events, or other people.
L2 MedInvites the respondent to draw on their own beliefs or experiences, but primarily to express rather than examine them.
L3 HighExplicitly encourages the respondent to examine, question, or revise their own assumptions, biases, mental models, or reasoning.

The question quadrant

A reference for the Openness criterion: questions sit on two axes — closed to open, and textual to intellectual.

(~Phil Cam, Question Quadrant)
08 The project

Belief Audit

The project is centred around a belief each student already holds, and what happens when they go looking for the strongest case against it. Individual work throughout, with small groups appearing as peer help (task 2) and critique (day 14).

The big question

What is a strongly held belief I have? What is the strongest argument against it?

Name your belief.Write down 3–5 beliefs you hold strongly — about success, relationships, education, society, or anything personally meaningful.
Choose your belief.In a small group, share your list; peers help identify which belief is most worth examining (usually the one that feels most obvious). Submit one sentence stating the belief you will investigate.
Write down what you think — and why.Before any research, privately write out why you hold this belief and the evidence or experience behind it. Keep it to yourself — you'll return to it at the end.
Go looking for the other side.Find the strongest arguments against your belief using all three: ask AI (brainstorm, but verify specific claims independently), talk to at least 2 people who push back, and find at least one social media post, article, or research paper that challenges it.
Keep a counter-argument log.For every counter-argument, log the argument in one sentence, the source, and the kind of evidence behind it — data, expert opinion, personal story, or no real evidence.
Steelman the other side.From your log, combine and sharpen the strongest counter-arguments into the single most compelling case against your belief — one a person who disagrees would recognise as fair and strong.
Evaluate.How strong is the steelman, really? Does it change your belief, complicate it, or leave it intact? Write a short personal evaluation of the strongest evidence against you and what you now think.
Present & note.Set up your work for a gallery walk (or private 1-on-1 session); others rotate through and ask about your belief, steelman, and evaluation, and you respond live. End with a quick private note: has your belief shifted, and why or why not?

Reflection questions

Meant to guide thinking at different points — not all at once. Students return to them as they work.

  • Which sources are you most likely to believe, and why?
  • What was the strongest argument you found against your belief?
  • What makes it the strongest — the evidence, or something else?
  • How did you decide which argument was strongest?
  • Did you change your mind? If yes, what moved you? If no, why not?
  • What have you learned from this exercise?
